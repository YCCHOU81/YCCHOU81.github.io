1. Spherical tori, marked poles, and unitary monodromy

The atlas computes the conformal structure of a spherical torus built by hemisphere gluing. A marked torus has periods 1 and τ, with Im τ > 0. A developing map gives projective monodromy by rotations of the round sphere. In the semisimple unitary families used here, we record one eigencharacter; choosing the other simultaneously inverts both multipliers.

Eτ=ℂ/(ℤ+τℤ)

The three-loop, double-edge, and tetrahedral seeds have weights (1,0,0,0), (1,1,0,0), and (1,1,1,1). They fit into the same four-point Treibich–Verdier construction. The undeformed three-loop seed has one conical point; its full decoration family can have additional conical points. The ordered weights correspond to these marked half-periods:

(p0,p1,p2,p3)=(0,12,τ2,1+τ2)
y″(z)=[∑j=03nj(nj+1)℘(z−pj;τ)+B]y(z)

Here B is the accessory parameter. A face Fᵢ of degree dᵢ in the labeled dual graph corresponds to an upstairs cone angle θᵢ. Degree counts repeated edge incidences, including both sides of a bridge.

di=2ni+1,θi=2πdi=(4ni+2)π

Weight zero is a smooth marked point upstairs. Thus (1,1,0,0) has two cone points of angle 6π, while (1,1,1,1) has four. Gauss–Bonnet gives total area 4π times the sum of the weights. The integer n in the classical family is not a substitute for the whole four-component weight vector.

Write N = n₀ + n₁ + n₂ + n₃. The dual graph has N vertices and describes N hemispheres on the downstairs sphere. Its double-cover torus has 2N hemispheres, hence area 4πN. The count shown by the repeated-decoration controls is the downstairs count N.

Why only pair insertion on the tetrahedron? Its vertices are trivalent, so they cannot be split into two blocks of at least two darts. It has no monogon to sprout. Pair insertion adds only bivalent vertices and does not make either operation available. The repeated tetrahedral menu therefore exposes six pair counts. The double-edge menu also retains its admissible local arm and cross forms. These labeled families do not exhaust all Treibich–Verdier weights or gluing graphs.

4. Double-edge and tetrahedral repeated counts

Choose Repeated decorations · choose counts in either interactive page. The entries describe the final labeled graph. Totals such as “three sprouts and two splits” would lose the attachment positions and cyclic partitions, so they are not a unique input.

Chains and reduced cores

Suppress intermediate bivalent vertices to obtain a reduced core. A stable edge carries a chain of multiplicity mₑ. Its smallest allowed multiplicity εₑ is one for an odd chain and two for an even chain; qₑ counts additional pairs. The ×m labels in the small website diagrams give the full chain length, not the number of pair insertions.

me=εe+2qe,εe∈{1,2},qe∈ℤ≥0

If the first edge has length ℓ, the chain alternates ℓ and 2π − ℓ. Thus each inserted bivalent vertex has perimeter 2π. The general face-incidence rule is

ni=nicore+∑eIieqe

Here Iᵢₑ counts the incidences of the edge in face Fᵢ; it is two when both sides belong to the same face. A pair consequently adds two to the total weight, including when both increments occur in one face.

Why counts do not shrink the source triangle

For the double-edge seed put P = 2π and use the positive lengths below. The source inequalities defining D give x, y, L > 0 and x + y + 2L = P.

x=P(1−R),y=P(R+2S−1)L=π(1−2S)

An odd arm has initial bridge length 2L and terminal loop π − L. An even arm alternates 2L and P − 2L and retains loop L. At A a cross uses y + L and x + L; the original loop becomes an ordinary edge of length L. The construction at B is analogous. Every first length and its P-complement remain positive throughout D.

For the tetrahedron, opposite edges have lengths x = P(1 − R), y = P(1 − S), z = P(R + S − 1), with x + y + z = P. They and their complements remain positive throughout T. Extending any of these chains therefore imposes no count-dependent positivity restriction. This statement concerns the character source; the corresponding τ-image can change shape substantially.

Mathematical counts and website limits. The graph rules allow unbounded nonnegative counts. The current controls use 0–32 per count, at most N = 144 and maximum face weight 72. These are computation limits, not failures of geometric admissibility. The displayed “hemisphere” total is N downstairs; the torus uses 2N hemispheres.

5. Three-loop: all admissible decorations

The three-loop seed has one six-valent vertex, three labelled loops ℓ₁, ℓ₂, ℓ₃, and face degrees (3,1,1,1). Its full decoration family can create additional conical points, so it is described by the ordered four weights, even though the seed itself is classical Lamé. The fixed cyclic order at the center is ℓ₁⁺, ℓ₁⁻, ℓ₂⁺, ℓ₂⁻, ℓ₃⁺, ℓ₃⁻.

The source metric stays positive

Use the capital geometric coordinates R,S. The open source triangle C and its three loop lengths are

C={0<R,S<12,R+S>12}u1=π(1−2R),u2=π(1−2S)u3=π(2R+2S−1),u1+u2+u3=π

Each loop has length strictly between zero and π. A loop appears twice at the central hemisphere, whose perimeter is therefore 2π. The boundary of C is excluded from point evaluation; the shaded starting triangle in the interactive pages is a compact barycentric inset.

A normal form for the complete family

  1. Choose the sprouted monogons. A subset Bspr of {1,2,3} records the sprouts in the reduced normal form. For i in Bspr, replace the original loop length uᵢ by a bridge bᵢ of length 2uᵢ and a terminal loop of length π − uᵢ. At the center, the bridge replaces the two darts of that loop. The new terminal hemisphere also has perimeter 2π. A sprout adds one downstairs hemisphere.
  2. Choose a noncrossing center dissection. After these sprouts, d = 6 − |Bspr| external darts remain in their inherited cyclic order. Number the gaps after the darts from 0 to d − 1. An admissible split separates consecutive blocks with at least two darts on each side. It is encoded by a polygon diagonal; multiple splits must be noncrossing. Their dual is the central ribbon tree. Every split adds a two-edge chain and two downstairs hemispheres.
  3. Choose a nonnegative pair count on every stable chain. The remaining loop chains and sprout bridges are odd, with smallest multiplicity one. The central split chains are even, with smallest multiplicity two. Extend each by qₑ pairs, giving mₑ = εₑ + 2qₑ. No further positivity condition depends on qₑ.

This is a normal form for the resulting labelled decorated graphs, rather than an unordered list of operations. Repeated operations are absorbed into the reduced core and the chain counts. It does not allow sprouting a face that is no longer a monogon, splitting nonconsecutive blocks, or crossing diagonals. The canonical construction uses sprouts, then center splits, then pair insertions; the original face labels and based marking are transported throughout.

For a split whose block has total external length ℓ, the two lengths are ℓ and 2π − ℓ. A proper block has 0 < ℓ < 2π throughout C. Subdivision alternates these positive lengths, so every inserted bivalent vertex retains perimeter 2π. The construction therefore gives the same source C for every finite count vector, although its τ-image changes.

Geometric versus analytic coordinates. At the original modulus τ the new-family evaluator uses (u,v) = (S′,R′), so its elliptic argument is u + vτ. Isogenies then change both the modulus and these parameters, as listed below. A change of eigenline may invert both multipliers without changing the zero equation.

The three-loop character triangle and its exact shifts

For every three-loop normal form the base source is C. Let κᵢ be one when the original monogon i is sprouted in the reduced normal form, and zero otherwise, for i = 1,2,3. Read n₀,…,n₃ from the final labelled face degrees. The ordinary-character half-shift is

2ΔR≡n0+n1−1+κ1 (mod 2)2ΔS≡n0+n2−1+κ2 (mod 2)

Choose each of ΔR,ΔS to be 0 or ½. The translated source is C + (ΔR,ΔS), and the analytic determinant receives (u,v) = (S + ΔS,R + ΔR). The κ correction records the antipodal change at a sprouted marked point; weight increments alone would give the wrong signs. All four half-shift classes occur. The shifts preserve projective monodromy, but neither these shifts nor the weights determine the full marked conformal branch.

For a fixed labelled three-loop graph G, its hemisphere metric and transported period basis determine a map

The full conformal image is ΦG(C). Explore shades only the sampled image of the compact source inset, while its paired open-edge paths supply resolved cusp tails. The determinant used for repeated graphs

At the original marked modulus, put (u,v) = (S + ΔS, R + ΔR). The repeated solver evaluates the generic Bloch-jet determinant, with ordered weights at the four marked half-periods. The jet indices at pᵢ have the parity of nᵢ − 1:

The entries use derivatives of a quasiperiodic elliptic kernel at differences of the marked half-periods; diagonal entries use its regular Laurent coefficients. Theta expansions evaluate these jets without expanding a large premodular polynomial. Modular reduction transforms both analytic characters and permutes the half-period weights; the answer is then returned to the original marking.

Isogeny shortcuts are used only with a calibrated character lift. In particular, q pairs on the selected t connector give weights (n,n,0,0), and q pairs on AB give (n,n,1,1), with n = q + 1. Both use the selected doubled-modulus lift T = 2τ and analytic parameters (S + q/2, R/2). The reduced weights are respectively (n,0,0,0) and (n,0,1,0), after the corresponding half-period relabeling. Equal weights in another labeled graph do not justify reusing that lift.

A zero equation does not identify the requested surface. Several roots can occur at the same character. The labeled gluing, its marking, a geometric period anchor, and continuation choose the branch. An arbitrary root search can return another surface with the same monodromy.

8. The stable numerical algorithm

Select the marked geometric branch

The labeled graph is expanded into hemisphere disks with the specified lengths and edge identifications. Cotangent finite-element energies produce two discrete harmonic one-forms. Derivatives of the developing holonomy recover a full integer period marking; it is checked to be unimodular. This retains more information than the two ordinary monodromy signs. For the forms in that oriented basis, set Gᵢⱼ = ∫ωᵢ ∧ *ωⱼ. The marked period is

τ=−G12+idetGG22

For a new configuration, two differently refined meshes give two approximate periods. Each is independently corrected to an analytic determinant root. The worker accepts the anchor only when those roots agree within its branch check; otherwise it reports the configuration unresolved. Previously checked graph-specific anchors and case-specific cached anchors avoid repeating this initialization. Two-mesh agreement is a numerical safeguard, not a proof of global uniqueness.

Continue the chosen root

  1. Keep the complete labeled counts. Construct the graph, validate its face degrees and metric cell, and apply its ordinary half-shift before evaluating analytic characters.
  2. Reuse a nearby point. Dragging transports the previous resolved root. Small tested configurations use ordinary floating-point correction; larger weights or unsuccessful corrections switch to decimal multiprecision.
  3. Subdivide unstable steps. A complex secant corrector is guarded against leaving the upper half-plane or moving too far. Boundary traces use finer internal steps than the displayed samples. For mixed graphs an intermediate geometric period check helps detect branch jumps.
  4. Increase precision as the cusp deepens. The working precision grows with the number of decimal orders approached and the largest weight. Exact decimal source strings keep a point distinct from the boundary even when binary floating-point would round its coordinate to one.
  5. Check the coordinates that matter. Repeating at higher precision compares the resulting τ and cusp coordinates. When a calibrated rational center is available, the small horizontal correction is checked relatively, rather than judging the much larger real coordinate alone.
  6. Return actual finite samples. The website stores decimal coordinates and transforms them before converting small drawing offsets to plotting numbers. A failed correction leaves a gap or ends the trace; it never inserts a predicted cusp endpoint.

The three-loop branch: graph anchor, elliptic matching, and stable cusp correction

The three-loop engine uses the same four-pole elliptic matching equation with the weights and character shift computed from its complete labeled graph. Its equation determines possible periods; the graph and its transported integer marking select the intended one. Two graphs can have the same weights and ordinary character signs yet select different roots and different rational horizontal limits. Cached anchors therefore retain the core and every stable-chain count.

  1. Build the exact graph. Expand the chosen normal form and pair counts. Check its four labeled face degrees, vertex count, positive edge lengths, and the full circumference at each hemisphere. The source point stays strictly inside the three-loop triangle.
  2. Initialize the marked period. A previously checked graph-specific anchor is analytically refined before reuse. For a new configuration, two different hemisphere meshes provide Hodge period estimates; independent corrections must reach the same elliptic root before the worker accepts an anchor. This is a numerical branch safeguard, rather than an interval proof of uniqueness.
  3. Continue and correct. Transport the previous resolved period in small source steps, subdividing when a correction moves too far. Dragging may use a fast preview; releasing the point requests a higher-precision correction. The worker retains the original marking instead of reducing the real part to a fixed strip.
  4. Use a stable residual. At each corrector, choose row and column scales and a complete-pivot ordering once, then keep them fixed throughout that solve. The final scalar Schur complement vanishes at the same nonsingular-pivot matching root. This avoids multiplying all small pivots into a determinant and avoids changing the residual normalization at every iterate.
  5. Resolve the cusp independently. The proved rational center and leading constant give an initial guess in the selected cusp chart. Guarded BigInt fixed-point Fourier/theta jets evaluate the full elliptic matching matrix, with extra digits for high derivatives and large weights. The asymptotic formula does not replace the corrected numerical point.
  6. Check both scales and the connection. Repeat the cusp correction at higher precision, compare the absolute chart coordinates, and compare the tiny horizontal displacement from its rational limit relatively. Check that the root stays in the marked phase sector. A completed trace also matches the independently corrected cusp tail to the branch continued from the marked graph anchor.
  7. Retain honest partial output. Large graphs can resolve several deep tail points before the intermediate path. Unresolved slots remain visible gaps, and the status distinguishes an overlap-checked trace from a locally refined marked-phase tail whose continuation overlap is still pending. No extrapolated endpoint or segment across a missing sample is drawn.
Three-loop timings and numerical scope

In local acceptance checks, a 91-point compact mesh for the center-split example took about 1.6 seconds. Two 21-point, twenty-decade traces with the same weights but distinct graph phases took about 2.2 seconds each; two fractional-residue examples reached sixteen decades in about 2.6 and 4.1 seconds. These are examples on the development machine, not a guaranteed time for every admissible count vector.

The count-32 stress test resolved three adjacent deep points at ε = 10⁻⁷⁸, 10⁻⁷⁹, and 10⁻⁸⁰ on each open-edge chart. Full 81-sample requests reached the roughly 45-second budget with only five to seven total samples resolved; the middle gaps and pending continuation overlap were retained. This demonstrates stable local deep-cusp correction, not completion of every count-32 trace. The controls currently limit each count to 32, the total downstairs hemisphere count to 144, and each face weight to 72.

The proved formulas hold for every fixed finite count vector and tangential positions in compact subintervals of an open source edge. They do not give a uniform error bound as counts grow while the source approaches the boundary. Source-vertex limits are not covered by this theorem or by the three-loop open-edge solver. Higher-precision agreement and relative displacement checks are numerical convergence evidence, not interval enclosures.

The double-edge and tetrahedral connector Fourier evaluator

Near a deep cusp, generic theta-jet formulas can subtract order-one terms whose difference is tiny. Merely asking for a small determinant residual can then accept cancellation or an underflowed zero. For the calibrated t-connector and AB-connector families approaching R = 1, the worker instead evaluates a Fourier form of the same reduced determinant.

The even part of the leading Kronecker-kernel term is simplified before taking jets or forming the matrix. This removes its identically irrelevant odd part and avoids recovering a small source distance by subtraction. Derivative rescaling and row/pivot normalization control the remaining dynamic range. These transformations retain the zero set; the normalized numerical residual need not be holomorphic.

The cancellation removed before evaluating even jets

For a scaled local coordinate z and small reduced-character displacement ξ, the trigonometric kernel term satisfies the exact even-part identity

Even[eiξz2sin(z2)]=isin(ξz)2sin(z2)

The irrelevant odd term has identically zero even derivatives. Computing the right-hand side’s small-ξ jets directly avoids subtracting nearly equal order-one jets on the left. The other Fourier terms are still evaluated, and precision and mode counts are increased to test the resulting root.

The Fourier solver repeats at higher precision and with twice as many modes. It checks the full coordinate absolutely and the small horizontal correction relatively. This special cancellation-free reduction currently applies to the two selected connector infinity families. Other source edges and mixed double-edge or tetrahedral decorations use the generic theta-jet evaluator at higher precision and guarded continuation; they do not inherit this connector fast path automatically. The three-loop engine uses the separate fixed-Schur method described above.

When a computation stops

A time stop retains its checked samples and a continuation checkpoint. Continue calculation uses the same labelled graph, exact source ray, and sampling grid while this page stays open. It can fill unresolved intermediate slots without repeating the earlier work. A changed case or path starts a new calculation. Precision or marked-branch failures remain explicit stops and are never repaired by drawing an unchecked continuation.

A cusp approximation can be unreliable before the terminal regime. In that situation the solver continues the actual marked graph branch and independently refines it, then tries the cusp shortcut farther along the path. Both the base and decorated roots must agree with the independently continued branches before cusp overlap is labelled matched. Reaching a deep target does not by itself resolve the whole intervening curve; the page reports the number of checked sample slots and leaves the other slots open.

Other-family computation, caching, and measured speed

All numerical work runs in browser workers on the visitor’s device. The static HTML site needs no remote computation service. The six seed/single-operation presets reuse stored 231-point compact images. Repeated count configurations build compact meshes on demand, with a smaller resolution cap for larger graphs. Case-specific anchors and completed traces can be reused; changing the source ray requires evaluating that ray.

On the development machine, tested nearby point updates took about 1–3 milliseconds after initialization. Small repeated compact meshes took roughly 0.6–1.6 seconds. Examples with nine boundary samples took about 7–16 seconds, depending on the family and cusp. These measurements exclude or vary with startup, rendering, cache state, and concurrent work; large mixed configurations can be substantially slower.

Separate count-32 checks on straight rays to the midpoints of the infinity edges reached ε = 10⁻⁸⁰ and retained horizontal corrections of order 10⁻¹⁶⁰. Those timing tests computed two deep nodes, not every sample of a full eighty-decade path. A deep target and high sampling density can exceed the page’s computation budget.

Why the graph formula selects the actual elliptic branch

In logarithmic developing-map coordinates, every punctured hemisphere is an exact flat half-cylinder for the global quadratic differential −(dF/F)². Rescaling by the shrinking edge length gives an actual inner half-plane surface. The main positive component has a node with 2Mᵢ sectors, so its annular modulus produces the coefficient 1/(πMᵢ) in the doubled torus period. Counting the other components gives exactly the correction N − Vᵢ, including trapped hemispheres.

The real resonant inner solution gives a reflection of the limiting metric itself. Its directed access sectors toward the labeled marks identify the complete phase Pᵢ. The original based cuts are transported through every untwisted attachment; retaining their integer winding gives the closed turn-vector formula instead of a phase only modulo 4. Exact elliptic sewing then identifies this graph phase with the marked period ratio. Finally, the resonant transmission residue supplies Lᵢ and the local matching law above.

The result concerns the canonical three-loop marking. A different basis of torus periods changes the coordinate by a modular transformation and changes how its rational horizontal center is displayed. The theorem does not establish injectivity of the full τ-image or classify every finite boundary arc.

11. Boundary paths and the two zoom scales

In the calibrated examples, an open source edge has a cusp limit, while approaching a source vertex can produce a direction-dependent finite limiting arc. Thus the τ-image is not obtained by connecting three sampled vertex values. To describe such an arc, approach the vertex with a fixed ratio between its two vanishing source distances, expand the premodular equation, and remove its common vanishing or singular factor. The leading coefficient gives a regularized equation for the finite limit.

The stored finite arcs belong to the six seed/single-operation presets: they sample 19 points per arc and stop before the unsampled ends. Arbitrary repeated configurations have no such precomputed boundary arcs; the compact mesh and requested source trace supply their picture. A regularized zero equation can contain additional branches, so neither stored arcs nor live traces certify the complete image boundary.

The slider ε is not the edge distance δ

The hollow circle chooses a compact starting point; the diamond chooses a boundary target. Limits follows the straight source segment

(R,S)(ε)=(1−ε)p∂+εpstart,0<ε≤1

At ε = 1 the point is the start, and ε → 0 approaches the target. For an edge target, δ(ε) is ε times the corresponding edge distance of the starting point. For example, approaching R = 1 gives δ(ε) = ε(1 − Rstart). Substituting ε directly for δ would shift the logarithmic intercept. For a vertex target in the other families the starting point fixes the direction, and the open-edge formulas above do not apply. The three-loop solver offers open-edge targets only. For example, its infinity edge has δ(ε) = ε(½ − Rstart).

What the overview and local graphs show

The TV images are shown in their original marked upper-half-plane representatives with Γ(2) reference guides; they are not folded into a single fundamental domain or clipped to the classical image region. The horizontal range expands to retain marked branches outside 0 ≤ Re τ ≤ 1, and the two separate Explore charts share that range. The overview uses compressed height Im τ/(1 + Im τ), with no change to the reported τ.

The two local plots use the selected ordinary coordinate τ or the cusp coordinate w. The first keeps equal coordinate units, making the near-vertical direction visible. The second magnifies the horizontal scale while retaining the vertical range. Both axes show absolute coordinates, including negative real w values. The decimal evaluator has a finite precision ceiling; the Fourier path also has a finite mode/precision budget. Repeating precision and doubling modes are convergence diagnostics, not interval enclosures. Two meshes can agree without establishing a theorem about every branch, and a determinant zero alone does not verify the requested gluing.

Stored decimal strings survive the cusp-coordinate transformation and origin subtraction. Only the resulting small screen offsets are converted to ordinary plotting numbers. Axis labels are rounded to the viewing scale; coincident printed labels do not imply an exactly constant real part.

Limits & Zoom offers a five-decade preview and a ten-decade comparison, each with one displayed sample per decade. Mixed double-edge and tetrahedral graphs start with the shorter preview because their branch continuation can be costly. The last three real parts, their distances to a reference when available, and coordinate-specific numerical checks remain visible. Use a deeper run when those values have not settled, especially for a large resonance M; 10⁻⁸⁰ is an optional research depth, not a routine accuracy requirement. The TV controls allow counts 0–32, N ≤ 144, and nᵢ ≤ 72. Final ε options extend through 10⁻⁸⁰. These choices are requests for a computation, not a guarantee that a whole high-density path will resolve. Compact meshes and traces have a nominal 45-second budget, and difficult cases retain their last resolved samples with a partial status. An unresolved step is not evidence that the corresponding surface does not exist.

Limits of the picture. Sampled shading is an image of the compact source inset, not the complete domain. Numerical branch agreement, precision repeats, and small residuals do not certify a full image boundary, a Jordan domain, global injectivity, or a new theorem about arbitrary hemisphere graphs. The three-loop all-count theorem above has its own geometric and analytic proof; numerical convergence checks do not supply that proof.
