MATHEMATICAL BACKGROUND

Geometry, monodromy, and computation

The atlas follows how hemisphere attachments change the conformal structure of a spherical torus while preserving its projective monodromy.

1. The geometric problem

A marked complex torus can be written using a period ratio τ in the upper half-plane:

E τ = ℂ / ( ℤ + τ ℤ ) , Im τ > 0 .

We equip this torus with a metric of Gaussian curvature 1 away from a single conical point, placed at 0. The cone angle measures the total angle around that point. This atlas focuses on the integer-index family

θ n = ( 4 n + 2 ) π , n ≥ 1 .

The spherical metric has a developing map into the round sphere. Continuing this map around loops produces rotations of the sphere: its projective monodromy lies in PSU(2), which is isomorphic to SO(3). A linear lift can be represented in SU(2).

The hemisphere construction selects a representative built from two isometric spherical triangles. The numerical output τ records the underlying conformal structure; it does not by itself specify the entire spherical metric.

2. Two triangles and one conical point

Start with a hemisphere whose boundary has three marked vertices. Its three interior angles are all π. Glue two copies using the torus edge pairings, so all six corner copies meet at one point. The resulting torus has cone angle 6π, corresponding to n = 1.

Label the triangle vertices P₁, P₂, P₃ and the opposite edges L₁, L₂, L₃, using the fixed cyclic labeling of the construction.

What one attachment does

Glue a hemisphere to a chosen edge of length ℓ, with 0 < ℓ < 2π, along a boundary arc of the same length. The complementary boundary arc becomes the new edge. Consequently:

  • The new edge has length 2π − ℓ.
  • The angle at each endpoint of that edge increases by π.
  • The area of the triangle increases by 2π.

Apply the matching operation to both triangles before forming the torus. One attachment count therefore adds area 4π and increases the cone angle by 4π.

After several attachments, the triangle is an abstract spherical disk with its own metric. Its developed image on the round sphere may overlap itself.

3. The attachment counts

The nonnegative integers (a, b, c) count attachments along L₁, L₂, L₃. Write α, β, γ for the angles at P₁, P₂, P₃.

Edge Count Angles increased by one attachment
L₁ a β and γ
L₂ b α and γ
L₃ c α and β
n = 1 + a + b + c α = ( 1 + b + c ) π β = ( 1 + a + c ) π γ = ( 1 + a + b ) π

All corners of both triangles meet at the conical point. The total angle and area are therefore

θ = 2 ( α + β + γ ) = ( 4 n + 2 ) π Area ( E ) = 4 n π .

The area identity also follows from Gauss–Bonnet: on a torus with curvature 1 away from one cone, area + 2π − θ = 0.

4. Monodromy parameters and the source triangle

The atlas uses real monodromy parameters (r, s), in its fixed period convention, in the open source triangle

0 < r < 12 , 0 < s < 12 , r + s > 12 .

For the attachment (a, b, c), the analytic character parameters used by the solver are

R = r + a+c 2 , S = s + b+c 2 .

The character parameters are periodic modulo integers. These half-integer shifts can change central signs in the linear lift while preserving the projective monodromy.

The shifted character does not determine the attachment branch. For example, (2,0,0), (0,2,0), and (0,0,2) all have n = 3 and give the same character modulo integers. They require separate geometric branch labels; the character alone cannot choose the requested attachment.

For each fixed triple, Explore varies (r, s) and plots the corresponding period ratios. Thus the displayed regions are conformal images in the τ-plane.

5. The analytic equation

The associated analytic problem is described by the Lamé equation

y″ (z) = [ n (n+1) ℘ (z;τ) + B ] y (z) ,

where ℘ is the Weierstrass elliptic function for periods 1 and τ, and B is an accessory parameter. The implementation computes τ using the associated premodular equation.

Let ζ be the Weierstrass zeta function. Its quasi-periods are η₁ = ζ(z + 1) − ζ(z) and η₂ = ζ(z + τ) − ζ(z). For σ = r + sτ, define

Z r,s (τ) = ζ (σ;τ) − r η1 − s η2 .

The first two premodular expressions are given below, with Z = Zᵣ,ₛ(τ) and all elliptic functions evaluated at (σ; τ):

Z r,s (1) = Z Z r,s (2) = Z3 − 3 ℘ Z − ℘′ .

Higher indices are evaluated by a recurrence. The base and attached period ratios satisfy

Z r,s (1) ( τbase ) = 0 Z R,S (n) ( τattached ) = 0 .

The superscript (n) labels the premodular expression; it is not an ordinary power. Finding a zero solves the analytic condition. The geometric starting point and continuation select which zero belongs to the chosen attachment.

6. Markings and the displayed domain

A change of period basis acts on τ by a fractional linear transformation. The subgroup Γ(2) consists of matrices in SL(2,ℤ) congruent to the identity modulo 2, and preserves the basis modulo 2.

The current plots use the right-hand chart

0 < Re τ < 1 , | τ − 12 | > 12 .

This is the right-hand half of a conventional Γ(2) fundamental domain. Keeping a consistent marking makes the change in period ratio visible. Stable modular transformations are also used internally to evaluate elliptic functions, with the result transported back to the chosen chart.

Explore compactifies the vertical display coordinate using y/(1 + y), where y = Im τ, so large heights fit on the screen. The local lens in Limits uses ordinary, uncompactified coordinate differences.

7. How the numerical algorithm works

Geometric starting points

Each supported attachment has a stored starting period ratio at a fixed interior monodromy point. These starting points were obtained from geometric meshes of the glued surface and refined using the analytic equation.

The geometric computation uses harmonic one-forms. Choose an oriented cycle basis and harmonic forms ω₁, ω₂ with respective periods (1,0) and (0,1). Their energy matrix is Qᵢⱼ = ∫ ωᵢ ∧ *ωⱼ. In this convention the period ratio is recovered by

τ = − Q12 + i detQ Q22 .

This Hodge calculation is part of the anchor precomputation. The browser reuses the stored anchors, so it can concentrate on analytic root correction and continuation.

Continuation and root correction

  1. Select the geometric branch. Load the anchor belonging to the full triple (a, b, c).
  2. Move through the source triangle. Change (r, s) in small steps, using the previous period ratio as the next initial guess.
  3. Correct the root. Apply damped complex Newton iteration to the relevant premodular equation. Reduce the step when correction becomes unstable or moves too far.
  4. Check the computation. Monitor the Newton correction and numerical consistency. The ordinary engine includes safeguards for cancellation and removable divisions in the recurrence.
  5. Draw resolved samples. Map an inset triangular mesh of source points to the τ-plane. Omit any face touching an unresolved sample.

The present bundle supports 1 ≤ n ≤ 12 and contains 364 geometric starting points. Their branch identifications are numerical checks, not interval certificates. The calculations run in background workers on the visitor’s device.

8. Boundary approaches and precise zoom

Limits uses normalized barycentric weights

( λ0 , λ1 , λ2 ) = ( 1−2s , 2 (r+s) −1 , 1−2r ) .

These weights are positive in the source triangle and sum to 1. An edge approach sets one weight to ε; the other two are (1−ε)t and (1−ε)(1−t). A vertex approach sets one weight to 1−ε; the other two are εt and ε(1−t). The remaining weights are taken in increasing index order.

The source boundary indices 0, 1, 2 label these weights. They are a separate labeling system from the attachment edges L₁, L₂, L₃.

Vanishing weight Source boundary
λ₀ → 0 s → 1/2
λ₁ → 0 r + s → 1/2
λ₂ → 0 r → 1/2

The parameter ε measures the chosen source approach, not distance in the τ-plane. The trend graph uses −log₁₀ ε horizontally. It displays real or imaginary parts relative to the last checked sample, or consecutive changes on a logarithmic vertical scale.

Precision near the boundary

The trace solver uses 70-digit working arithmetic and checks each accepted sample at 100 digits. It retains decimal coordinates and subtracts the selected origin before converting small offsets to ordinary plotting numbers. This avoids losing tiny differences by first rounding two nearby absolute coordinates.

The shaded interior mesh uses ordinary precision and is hidden at sufficiently fine zoom. The checked trajectory remains available. A trace stops if the next point cannot be verified or its continuation budget is reached; already checked samples are retained.

Coordinates adapted to cusps

The original τ coordinates remain available. Two additional views can reveal behaviour near 0 or 1:

w0 = 1 − 1τ , w1 = 1 1−τ .

Changing this view changes the plotted coordinates, while retaining the same selected attachment branch. The choice of source edge alone does not identify the attached branch’s exact limiting cusp.

A final computed sample is not an exact limiting value. Consecutive changes are not errors from an assumed limit. The displayed error figures are numerical diagnostics; the plots do not prove an asymptotic formula, certify the true image boundary, or establish global injectivity.
